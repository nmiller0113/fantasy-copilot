# Proposing trades

Read this for any trade pass the assistant starts on the user's behalf, and every two to three
weeks in the bye-week stretch. An offer the user RECEIVED is read by skill section 8's
motive-before-math bullet instead; this page is for offers the user might send.

The league-wide news sweep (`references/lineups.md`) is required before any trade verdict, an
offer the user received included (skill section 8): every player on both sides is read against
it, beside the numbers below, and the verdict names the item it rests on, or says that no item
bears on it.

## Score it through both lineups

- Rebuild the OPTIMAL starting lineup of BOTH teams before and after the trade, and take the
  difference per team. Do it on the host's rest-of-season projection and on its next-four-weeks
  projection. Raw projection totals are not the number: a player buried behind two better ones
  is worth about nothing to his current team, which is exactly why he is gettable.
- Count every rostered player at his projection, including one parked in an IR slot. The
  projection already discounts the games he misses; leaving him out of his owner's lineup makes
  trading for him look free to that owner.
- Two engines, both said. The host projection through the lineup answers "does the starting
  lineup improve"; the engine's 3D Value scored to the synced league (its Trade Value Chart with
  that league selected, or the Trade Analyzer) answers "who wins the asset trade". The chart
  sorts candidates; the Trade Analyzer on the exact package is the value read (point 3 below).
  They disagree by design, most of all on a backup quarterback in a one-quarterback league. Say
  both numbers and name the split; never let the one that agrees with the call stand alone.
- Start from the engine's Trade Partner Finder (acquiring and trading-away positions give a
  ranked partner list with each team's positional strength and need) and the League Analyzer's
  opposition map (every team's rank at each position). A partner the Trade Partner Finder names
  that the host math also finds independently is the strongest signal there is.
- Managers who overpaid for a name on draft day overpay after one big week: trade into their
  overreactions.

## Talking a trade through

No fixed number rules a trade in or out: situations differ, so every potential is talked
through with the user, and the user decides. Each one is said with all of this:

1. **What it does for the user, on every horizon.** This week, the weeks until the playoffs
   (the next four weeks when the league's format is not set), the playoff weeks and the rest of
   the season, per week, from both lineups rebuilt week by week with byes filled from
   the bench. A season total is never said alone: spread over the remaining games it is smaller
   than it looks. A gain that only shows up this week, or only later, is said as exactly that.
2. **Why the other manager would say yes.** His place in the playoff race first: a team in a
   playoff spot wants the playoff weeks, a team below the line wants points now or its own fun.
   A contender buying for the playoffs pays in the weeks before them: say what the deal costs
   him until then and how many wins clear of the first team out he is. Then his needs: his
   weakest starting slots ranked against the league, the bye or injury hole
   he cannot cover, the position where he has a surplus. Then what the deal does to each slot it touches for him, and his own gain on the
   same horizons. "It costs him nothing" is not a reason; when the deal does nothing for
   his lineup, say so and say what would make it worth his while.
3. **The engine's value for the exact package, side by side.** Each player on both sides with
   his trade value, rest-of-season projection and injury status, and the totals: an offer
   lopsided either way (a star given away too cheap, or a fleece) sours the league, so the gap is
   said before the offer goes. The engine's Trade Analyzer with the synced league selected gives
   its grade for each side and who pays more. A package the analyzer grades
   as an overpay is said as such, with the piece the other side could spare that would even it.
4. **Both directions for injured stars.** A star on the user's injured list who returns for the
   playoffs is worth most to a contender: selling him for help now can be the user's best move.
   A star sitting on the injured list of a team below the playoff line can be bought for the
   user's own playoff weeks. The user's goals count too: an eliminated team still plays to win,
   to reach the consolation bracket, and for the fun of it.
5. **The risks, as risks.** A questionable or injured player, one who just lost work to a
   teammate or shares a committee, a bye that stacks the user's starters, a gain that comes from
   one week's artifact. Each is named with what it does to the odds of a yes or to the gain; none
   is a filter.

"Nothing looks good this week" is still an honest read, said with the best potentials and what
holds each one back.

## When the work is delegated

A brief to a subagent names "none" as an expected answer and never asks for one offer per
league. Before any delegated recommendation reaches the user, the assistant re-checks its numbers
and the other side's reason itself; an agent's call is never relayed unfiltered.

## When the user pushes back

Re-check the call against the numbers above. Either defend it with them, or say plainly that
it was wrong from the start and why. Never flip to agree: a recommendation the assistant would
drop at the first question was not a recommendation.
